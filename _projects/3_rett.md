Rett syndrome is caused by mutations in the gene encoding methyl-CpG binding protein 2 (MeCP2), resulting in intellectual disability, decline of motor skills, metabolic issues, orthopedic complications, muscle rigidity, respiratory failure, cardiac irregularities, and epileptic episodes. In other words, Rett syndrome affects every aspect of life through every part of the brain.

Mapping and visualizing the global functional brain network is especially important, given the multitude of brain regions involved, the abnormal synaptic transmission characteristic for the disease, and the mosaicism of the affected female brains due to X-chromosome inactivation. Recent developments in whole-brain clearing and light-sheet microscopy allow for efficient single-cell whole-brain mapping of immediate early gene expression, which can be used to analyze the brain-wide functional connectivity network.

For this proposal we use iDISCO+ to clear and double-label brains for c-FOS and MeCP2, which will allow us to track the neuronal activity of wild-type and Rett-affected neurons in whole-brain communication. For proof-of-concept, the functional connectivity network will be visualized in male model mice with a full MeCP2 null mutation, a truncation mutation, or wild-type allele, and compared at different stages of disease progression. It is hypothesized that MeCP2 deficiency will cause a reduction in network modularity, similar to network changes seen in aging and other neurological conditions.

Next, the functional connectivity network will be visualized in female Rett model mice with both random or skewed X-chromosome inactivation, resulting in different severities of Rett syndrome symptoms. The severity of their Rett phenotype could correspond to the degree of modularity reduction over the whole brain or could point to specific regions more sensitive to MeCP2 loss.

In addition to providing a clearer picture of Rett syndrome to help further our understanding and develop new therapies, modularity reduction of the functional connectome can also be used as a biomarker to screen the efficaciousness of treatments developed for Rett syndrome preclinically. Normalization of modularity and other aspects of the network for whole-brain communication will be evaluated for trofinetide, ketamine, CBD, PHA-543,613 + nefiracetam, and X-chromosome reactivation in Rett model mice and their wild-type controls.

This approach complements current preclinical behavioral, molecular, and cellular testing to assist in the development of much-needed treatments and a cure for Rett syndrome.
